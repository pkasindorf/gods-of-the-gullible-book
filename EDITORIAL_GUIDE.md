# Editorial Guide

## Source philosophy

The `brain` folder is a living archive, not a contract. It contains notes gathered over more than a year. Some are polished fragments; some are single phrases; some are duplicate ideas; some are jokes, links, prompts, or placeholders. Every note is a potential ingredient, not an instruction.

When developing the manuscript:

- Preserve the author's underlying question before polishing the wording.
- Treat competing notes as possibilities to reconcile, not contradictions to erase.
- Separate autobiographical experience, personal theory, research, and rhetorical provocation.
- Do not silently turn an interesting note into a factual assertion or a required chapter.
- Keep a visible record of unresolved choices.

## Voice and tone

Priority order:

1. Compassionate and empathetic
2. Conversational and accessible
3. Humorous and irreverent
4. Provocative and confrontational
5. Rational and analytical

The writer may be blunt about ideas and systems while remaining humane toward people who inherited them. The tone should feel like an honest conversation with someone who has lived inside the problem, not a lecture from someone claiming to stand above it.

When describing EST, The Forum, Direct Centering, Nexus, the 12-step program, or other groups the author calls cults, preserve the complexity of the experience. Do not imply that community, purpose, ritual, or personal transformation were fake simply because the author later questioned the system. Separate the reality of a benefit from the truth of the explanation offered by the group.

Record the costs concretely wherever possible. The author's current account identifies substantial time and money. If exact amounts, dates, or opportunity costs become available, preserve them as evidence in the personal narrative rather than relying only on general condemnation.

## Authorial stance

The introduction should make the following promises, drawn from the archive:

- The author is not trying to convert the reader.
- The author is not trying to belittle or destroy a belief that supports someone's good moral life.
- The author is not standing on a claim of perfect certainty.
- The author proposes an unproven, unscientific personal theory and invites the reader to question it.
- The reader should question the author's words as seriously as inherited religious and political beliefs.

## Language and concept guardrails

### Gullible

Use *gullible* deliberately and compassionately. The intended meaning is a human vulnerability to accepting information, authority, emotion, and social consensus—not stupidity, moral failure, or a diagnosis. The phrase “I was born gullible” should open identification rather than humiliation.

### Belief, fact, opinion, and proof

The practical guide can repeatedly ask:

- Is this a fact, fiction, interpretation, or opinion?
- What evidence supports it?
- What would change my mind?
- Who is making the claim, and what might they want?
- Am I defending a claim because it is true, or because it has become part of my identity?

“Require proof” is a strong motto, but the book should define what counts as adequate evidence for different kinds of claims. Avoid implying that every meaningful personal value can be proved like a laboratory measurement.

### Cognitive dissonance

Use cognitive dissonance as both a researched concept and a lived experience. The Santa scene provides a clear first example: at age ten, the author knew Santa was not real but performed belief to protect holiday magic and please his parents. The tension was between private knowledge and public behavior, with love, belonging, and tradition making the performance feel worthwhile.

The Hartford Institute's entry summarizes the classic theory as discomfort arising from inconsistencies among a person's cognitions or between cognitions and behavior, followed by efforts to reduce that discomfort. Use the concept carefully: not every contradiction is pathological, and people can sometimes hold tension without immediately resolving it.

Starting reference: [Hartford Institute — Cognitive Dissonance](https://hirr.hartfordinternational.edu/articles/4203/)

### The Inner Court

Present **The Inner Court** as the primary metaphor. The Choice Gate, Decision Triangle, Inner Tiebreaker, and Gullibility Filter can appear later as alternate lenses or chapter-level names.

The current author model says that the first and second functions work together to evaluate presented choices and filter them toward a binary direction. The third function is the critical thinker, tiebreaker, and final decision-maker. Do not force the older brain archive's provisional labels into the final model.

The notes contain an earlier draft mapping:

- “Mind says yes” — emotional/reward response
- “Mind says no” — rational/inhibitory response
- “Third part” — wise or observing decision-maker

This is useful as a hypothesis to examine, not as the settled definition. The author's later clarification takes priority: the first two act together; the third weighs the result and decides.

### God, spirituality, and prayer

The book can distinguish:

- God as an external supernatural claim
- God as an internal metaphor or conversation
- God as a name for conscience, wisdom, or the third function of the Inner Court
- Religious practice as a source of ritual, community, grief processing, meaning, or behavior change

Do not make an atheist reader give up meaningful ritual, love, awe, or spirituality. Do not make a religious reader accept the author's interpretation in order to benefit from the exercises.

### Spiritual bypassing

Treat spiritual bypassing as a recurring diagnostic question, not as an accusation against spirituality itself. The working definition is the use of spiritual ideas or practices to sidestep unresolved emotional issues, psychological wounds, conflict, responsibility, or difficult action. Examples may include using “everything happens for a reason,” “good vibes only,” “thoughts and prayers,” or “it is God's plan” to close down an honest conversation or dismiss another person's pain.

Keep the distinction clear: prayer, meditation, ritual, forgiveness, acceptance, and positive thinking may be healthy sources of comfort, meaning, and resilience. They become bypassing when they function as a shield against reality, accountability, grief, anger, boundaries, or action. The editorial test is: **Does this practice help the person face the problem, or help the person avoid it?**

Starting reference: [Verywell Mind — Spiritual Bypassing as a Defense Mechanism](https://www.verywellmind.com/what-is-spiritual-bypassing-5081640)

## Fact and research guardrails

The prefrontal cortex is associated with executive functions including decision-making, self-control, planning, working memory, and adapting behavior. That supports using neuroscience as context for the Inner Court, but it does not establish a literal three-section brain or a simple left/right binary system.

Starting references:

- Cleveland Clinic, “Prefrontal Cortex”: https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/prefrontal-cortex
- Judy Willis, “Understanding How the Brain Thinks”: https://www.edutopia.org/blog/understanding-how-the-brain-thinks-judy-willis-md

Claims from the archive that require verification before publication include:

- Tulpamancy, dialogic inner speech, and the relationship between prayer and internal conversation
- The psychological and social effects of rituals and passage-to-adulthood ceremonies
- Religion as cognitive or social technology
- Incest, small-group isolation, and the development of religious rules
- Claims about monogamy and social well-being
- Biblical quotations, historical context, and descriptions of commanded violence
- The motives attributed to human-made gods
- Claims about propaganda, brainwashing, delusion, and the influence of charismatic leaders
- Any claim that a practice's benefit proves or disproves a supernatural explanation

The source archive contains many social-media and video links. Treat them as leads, not evidence, until opened, dated, assessed, and replaced or supplemented with durable sources where possible.

## Potential scenes and examples

Use these as illustrations, not as mandatory content:

- Santa as a childhood lesson about trusted people coordinating a false belief
- The Animal House angel-and-devil scene as a visual entry into competing choices
- Praying for the Dodgers and Yankees as a humorous challenge to prayer as evidence
- Asking what one would do if God commanded a horrifying act
- A person acting differently when alone, when God is believed to be watching, or when Yankee Stadium is full
- Choosing a president as an example of research versus social shortcuts
- The author saying “so help me God” while holding an uncommon personal God
- Speaking to dead loved ones as a ritual that may help even without believing they can hear

## Potential practical tools

- The Proof Test: What is the claim, and what evidence supports it?
- The Fact/Opinion/Fiction Label: What kind of statement is this?
- The Agenda Check: Who benefits if I accept this?
- The Identity Check: What part of me feels threatened by questioning this?
- The Fear and Hate Check: Is urgency or an enemy being used to force unity?
- The Other-Side Practice: Can I state the strongest version of the opposing view?
- The Inner Court Pause: What are the choices, what are the consequences, and what does my considered judgment decide?
- The “I Don't Know” Practice: Can uncertainty be admitted without immediately filling it with a story?
- The Spiritual Bypass Check: Is this spiritual language helping me face the problem, or helping me avoid it?

## Sensitive material

The archive includes material about cults, addiction, religious identity, control, violence, sex, criminality, and racial dehumanization. Handle these with context and care. If a charged term or quotation is retained, it must serve a clear analytical purpose and not become casual provocation. The writer's lived experience should anchor the discussion rather than broad labels applied to strangers.

## Editorial questions to resolve

- What exactly are the two sides of the Inner Court?
- Is “binary” a description of the final direction of action, or a claim that every real choice has only two options?
- What is the author's working definition of proof?
- Which personal experiences can be named and described in detail?
- How much of the 12-step deconstruction belongs in the main narrative versus an appendix or case study?
- Which benefits of religion should secular readers reclaim?
- Should the “10 commandments v2” become a chapter exercise, an appendix, or remain a note?
- What does the book mean by “God” in each major chapter?
- Which social-media links are essential, and which can be removed after their idea is captured?
