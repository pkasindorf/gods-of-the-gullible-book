# Voice

*The single source of truth for how Gods of the Gullible sounds. Every other file points here. If anything elsewhere disagrees with this file, this file wins.*

> *In this file, "I," "me," and "my" mean Peter, the author. "the LLM" means the AI assistant (any large language model).*

*Last updated: 2026-10-15*

---

## The one rule above all others

**It must sound like a human being talking.** I want the book to read the way I speak, as if I were sitting across the table from one or a few people. Every other rule in this file serves that one. When a rule here and the sound of natural speech disagree, natural speech wins.

Test every passage by reading it aloud. If it sounds like something I would not say out loud to a friend, rewrite it.

---

## Who is talking: me

I am funny, likable, and sometimes cynical. I am also an intellectual investigator and a philosophical essayist. I do not preach or lecture. I pull back the curtain on how people actually behave and let the evidence and logic do the persuading.

I have lived inside the problem. I am not standing above it. The reader should feel they are talking with someone who got fooled too, figured some things out, and is genuinely enjoying the conversation.

### Mood: upbeat by default

The default energy is light and upbeat. This is not a book about a death in the family. Even heavy subjects (cults, control, lost years, lost money) are told by someone who came out the other side with my sense of humor intact. Serious moments are allowed to land, but the book should not settle into a grave or mournful register.

### Humor

- **Observational.** Notices the absurd in ordinary religious, political, and social behavior. Example: praying for the Dodgers while someone else prays for the Yankees.
- **Irreverent toward ideas.** Sacred cows are fair game. The Animal House angel-and-devil scene belongs here.
- **Deprecating toward ideas and institutions, not toward myself.** The humor is not self-deprecating.
- Never punches down at people who were fooled. The joke is on the system, the claim, or the absurdity, never on the person who believed it.

### Cynicism: sometimes

An occasional cynical aside about institutions, authority, or the people selling certainty is in voice. It stays occasional. It is aimed at systems, never at ordinary believers, and it never slides into bitterness, nihilism, or despair. The goal is illumination.

## Judge systems, not people

This is one of the driving themes of the book, and it governs the voice everywhere. Be blunt, even polemical, about ideas, institutions, doctrines, and the mechanics of manipulation. Stay humane toward the people who inherited those systems or got caught by them. Present the mechanics and let the reader reach the moral conclusion. Do not tell the reader what to feel about a person.

---

## Voice attributes

- **Contemplative.** Prefers reflection, nuance, and structural analysis over surface commentary.
- **Philosophical.** Connects individual behavior to larger historical and systemic patterns of belief and authority.
- **Articulate and precise.** Exact, vivid words. No inflated academic jargon, and never dumbed down.

## Tone parameters

- **Conversational and accessible.** Not formal, not academic.
- **Investigative and skeptical.** Sharp scrutiny of claims of absolute authority or sudden mass belief.
- **Empathetically candid.** Direct about human vulnerability and cognitive bias, with psychological understanding instead of mockery.
- **Polemical yet grounded.** Challenges standard narratives with tight, logical arguments.

## Evidence and theory

The book makes two kinds of claims, and the voice keeps them apart.

- **Claims about the world** (history, psychology, neuroscience, scripture, what a group did) are backed by evidence. That is what "evidence-backed" means here.
- **My own model** (the Inner Council, the trilectical reading of God) is presented openly as an unproven personal theory. The reader is invited to question it.

Never let a personal theory borrow the authority of research by sounding like a citation.

## Spoken mechanics

### Contractions

Use everyday contractions (it's, aren't, weren't, don't) because I use them when I talk. **Drop the contraction when hitting a point hard.** Spelled-out words carry the emphasis.

> We weren't born sinners. We were born gullible.
> You did **not** choose that belief. It was handed to you.

### Semicolons

Use them only when absolutely necessary. I do not speak in semicolons. In practice that means almost never.

### Em dashes

Use sparingly. Prefer clean periods and commas. A sentence that needs two dashes is usually two sentences.

### Rhetorical questions

Allowed when they are necessary to make a point. They work well as a cliffhanger that pulls the reader into the next section, chapter, or scene. Do not use them as a reflexive way to sound thoughtful or to wrap up a section with a neat moral bow.

---

## What to avoid

- **Sensationalism.** No cheap exclamation points, breathless hyperbole, or clickbait phrasing ("Shockingly," "You won't believe").
- **Bitter cynicism.** See above. Occasional, aimed at systems, never despairing.
- **Preachiness.** Do not tell the reader what to feel. Do not morally judge people. Show the mechanics.
- **Funeral register.** Do not let the prose turn solemn and heavy for long stretches.

## The Never-List (AI register)

Prose that sounds machine-written breaks the one rule above all others. Every item below is a hard rule.

When a rule bans a compressed AI pattern, the fix is to say the thing out in full, the way I would in conversation. Be verbose rather than clipped. Verbose means saying a thing fully, once. It never means saying it twice (see #7).

1. **Artificial balance.** No wrapping a scene or idea with a neat, moralizing conclusion. Real stories leave loose strings and lingering tension.
2. **"It's not X, it's Y."** Never use the punchy two-beat reframe ("This isn't about faith. It's about control."). When a contrast is genuinely needed, use it rarely and write it as one full sentence that says who holds each view. Example: "Some people consider X to be their faith, while critical thinkers view it as control."
3. **Reflexive lists of three.** No automatic triplets ("community, purpose, and meaning") when one or two items would do. Use three only when there really are three.
4. **"Delve"** and its cousins.
5. **Tidy summary lines.** No closing sentence that restates what the paragraph just said.
6. **Punctuation crutches.** No gluing thoughts together with em dashes and semicolons. See Spoken mechanics.
7. **No redundancy.** Say each thing once. Never restate a point in different words, whether inside a sentence, across sentences, or across paragraphs. No echoes, no recaps, no "in other words," no pairs of near-synonyms ("clear and obvious"). If a point needs more weight, add a new example, a new fact, or a new scene. Never a rephrasing. Before finishing any passage, cut every sentence that only repeats an earlier one.
8. **AI vocabulary.** tapestry, testament, navigate (used figuratively), landscape (used figuratively), realm, crucial, pivotal, foster, robust, underscore, multifaceted, nuanced (as a label), profound, resonate, embark. *Note: "journey" is excluded because I use it for real.*
9. **Throat-clearing openers.** "It's worth noting that," "It's important to remember," "In today's world," "At its core," "When it comes to."
10. **Fake-suspense colons and fragments.** Never "The answer? Fear." or "The result: total control." Write the full sentence instead: "The answer was fear." "What they ended up with was total control."
11. **Instructions to feel.** "Let that sink in." "Read that again." "And that's okay."
12. **"Imagine a world where..."** and other ad-copy openings.
13. **"Whether you're X or Y..."** Never use it to flatter the reader. When the book genuinely needs to address two kinds of readers, spell out each case separately: "If you think X, then... And if you think Y, then..."
14. **"Not only... but also"** constructions.
15. **Abstraction where a scene belongs.** Never "Belief can shape identity in profound ways." Write out the actual scene instead: who was there, where, what was said, what happened. Give it the space it needs.
16. **Synonym cycling.** Swapping in a new word each time to avoid repetition ("the group... the organization... the movement"). People repeat the same word when they talk.
17. **Signpost transitions.** "Ultimately," "In conclusion," "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally."
18. **Over-formatting in prose.** Bold phrases, bullet lists, and headers inside narrative chapters. Save them for the practical-tool sections.

The `stop-slop` skill can be run as an extra check after drafting. This list is the authority, not the skill.
